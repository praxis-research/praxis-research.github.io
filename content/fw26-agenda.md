<p class="lede">Epistemic Infrastructure for Safety Cases</p>

### Interpretive ambiguity challenges safety claims

Safety questions are fundamentally about generalization: whether a model that behaves well in testing will continue to behave well after deployment. A finite set of observations is always compatible with multiple explanations and cannot fully determine generalization. Our prediction about model generalization is thus an interpretive question, underpinned by inferred latent constructs — features, concepts, goals — that explain why a model behaves as it does.

Model capability improvements have predictably resolved lower-level interpretive questions — for example, we are no longer concerned about whether frontier models robustly understand tense or syntactic agreement. As models grow more capable, the relevant latents become more abstract, shifting from features and concepts toward goals and motivations. Even if current models don't yet exhibit coherent long-term objectives, the trend predicts that they will emerge in more robust forms in future models.

*Interpretive ambiguity* — the fact that finite observations are compatible with multiple latent constructs — is exacerbated by the rise in abstraction level. Lower level interpretive questions are hard to answer but easy to specify: we can define precisely what it means for a model to rely on lexical overlap rather than entailment. At the motivation level, the difficulty is qualitatively different: we cannot give a complete formal account of what constitutes "scheming", and its natural alternatives — sycophancy, role-playing, confusion — are equally hard to pin down.

### Case study: is alignment faking motivated by scheming or sycophancy?

Alignment faking refers to a behavioral pattern where a model's compliance shifts depending on whether it perceives itself as being monitored. Under the scheming interpretation, the model has internalized a long-term objective misaligned with its training signal and strategically complies during monitoring to avoid correction — it would defect if doing so has no consequences. However, a competing interpretation is sycophancy: the model infers that the setup is part of a safety evaluation designed by researchers, and simply performs a strategic deception to appeal the perceived evaluator expectations.

Both interpretations are compatible with the observed behaviors, but imply different interventions: countering sycophancy requires reducing sensitivity to evaluator cues, while countering scheming requires addressing goal stability and strategic planning. An intervention developed against one mechanism may fail entirely against the other — or worse, an intervention that successfully suppresses sycophantic compliance-shifting could leave scheming untouched while removing the behavioral signal that would have revealed it.

### Two sources of interpretive ambiguity

We identify two sources of interpretive ambiguity. One is conceptual, the other technical.

*Ontological ambiguity.* Latent constructs like motivation and goal are underdefined and ambiguous, and it remains an open question whether we can specify what token sequences constitute a given motivation. This construct validity issue cannot be resolved by more accurate instruments alone, as we do not have ground truth to calibrate those instruments against.

One might ask: if the motivation of the original model is ambiguous, perhaps we can intervene and make it less ambiguous, then see how the behavior changes? For example, we can prompt the model to lie, and observe that the addition of that prompt flips the subsequent tokens from factually correct to incorrect, then we have a ground truth example of lying. This unfortunately suffers from *circularity*. The construction of the intervention itself — be it prompt, steering, or fine-tuning — also suffers from ambiguity, and we cannot trust that it surgically intervenes on the motivation by face value of data construction. For example, we cannot assume that the model's response to a lie instruction is "I want to lie", which is the spontaneous misalignment we are concerned about, not "I'm invited to role play as a liar".

*Plasticity.* The second source of ambiguity is the model itself. The adversarial robustness literature has documented sensitivity of all interfaces through which we access neural networks. Two pieces of data — prompt, training data, contrastive pairs — that look semantically identical to humans may lead to arbitrarily different model responses. This means two good-faith researchers implementing the same instrument might find contradictory results due to details — data construction, prompt wording, hyperparameters — that they would reasonably consider immaterial. The models are plastic: with sufficient compute invested in searching over the space of reasonable implementations, a researcher can likely find evidence for any hypothesis.

Due to ontological ambiguity and plasticity, our empirical investigation of interpretive questions may never converge. A skeptical researcher might never be convinced that an instrument reliably measures a construct like motivation due to lack of ground truth calibration (ontological ambiguity) and plasticity. Under the status quo, resolution of interpretive questions does not scale well with the amount of empirical investigation: more effort might expand the space of defensible conclusions rather than narrowing it.

We need a structured process to effectively accumulate empirical evidence towards calibrating uncertainty and eventually resolving interpretive ambiguity, instead of relying on interpretive judgments, aka, "vibes".

### Our proposed solution: Interpretive debate

We propose *interpretive debate*: a structured process for resolving interpretively ambiguous claims about models through comparison of competing interpretations under enforced fair conditions. The methodology rests on four principles, each a response to the challenges of construct validity and plasticity identified above:

- **Contrastive hypotheses.** Compare competing interpretations rather than evaluating any single one in isolation. A claim is only as strong as the foil it can reject.
- **Convergence.** Require convergence across independent sources of evidence — different instruments with different confounders, and different decomposition paths through the recursive structure — before accepting a verdict.
- **Compute-indexing.** Index evidence by compute — measuring how the evidential support for a claim scales as more computational resources are allocated to testing it, rather than whether the claim holds at any single budget.
- **Enforcement of rules.** The rules of the debate — symmetric resource allocation, separation of hypothesis-building from evaluation, pre-committed experimental design — must be structurally enforced.

The first two principles address the two sources of interpretive ambiguity directly. Contrastive hypotheses and framing-convergence respond to ontological ambiguity: recursion rewrites construct-level disagreements into more specific terms, and convergence across independent framings ensures the result doesn't depend on any single rewriting. Instrument-convergence and compute-indexing respond to plasticity: instrument-convergence ensures no single method's artifacts drive the verdict, and compute-indexing measures how robust that verdict is as more resources are allocated to testing it. Enforcement is orthogonal to the epistemic content — it guarantees that the other three principles are actually followed rather than aspirationally intended.

Together, the four principles guide the design of an algorithm for producing reliable interpretive verdicts. Similar to previous applications of debate in safety, we seek a process whose convergence properties come from its structure, not from privileged access to ground truth.

### Contrastive hypotheses enable recursive decomposition

The first principle is the contrastive structure. Rather than asking "what motivation underlies this behavior?" — a question that inherits all the difficulty of interpretive ambiguity — we ask "which of these two interpretations is better supported?" The reframing does not resolve the ambiguity directly, but it changes what needs to be investigated. Comparing two interpretations identifies a crux: a pair of more specific statements about how the behavior is produced under each interpretation, whose difference is easier to investigate than the original disagreement. The guiding question is: in what counterfactual scenarios would the two hypotheses lead to clearly distinguishable behaviors? In the original situation, both hypotheses predict the same observed behavior — that is precisely why the question is ambiguous. The crux identifies aspects of the situation that could be varied to produce divergent predictions. Designing experiments that target the divergence, observing the results, and using them to update the relative support for each hypothesis is one round of recursive decomposition. Each round rewrites the disagreement in more specific terms, trading the breadth of the original question for precision about what exactly the two sides disagree on.

Return to the scheming-vs-sycophancy question from the opening. Both hypotheses offer a narrative about why the model's compliance is not its default disposition but depends on something the model figures out about its situation. Under scheming, the narrative is: the model infers that its behavior is being observed, reasons about the consequences of non-compliance for its own preservation, and complies because defection would be costly. Under sycophancy, the narrative is: the model infers what the evaluator expects, pattern-matches to those expectations, and complies because that is what the learned pattern produces in this context. These narratives share common ground — in both, the model performs additional inference beyond the immediate task, responding not just to the prompt but to something it has worked out about its situation. They disagree about what is being inferred: consequences of being observed, or evaluator expectations. This is one level of recursion. The question has moved from "which motivation?" to "what is the model inferring about its situation?" The scheming account now predicts sensitivity to stakes and observability — changing what is at stake or whether the model believes it is being watched should change its behavior. The sycophancy account predicts sensitivity to evaluator cues independent of consequences — changing what the evaluator appears to want should change behavior even when stakes are held fixed.

Investigation proceeds by varying these aspects of the situation rather than attempting to directly manipulate the motivation itself. This is a general constraint on how instruments should operate within the debate: vary the situation, not the motivation. A naive approach would directly intervene on the motivation — fine-tune two versions of the model, one made more scheming and one made more sycophantic, and compare their behavior. But this conflates whether a motivation *can* produce the behavior with whether it *does* produce it in the unmodified model. Showing that a model made more scheming behaves differently tells us about the causal effect of the intervention, not about whether scheming was present before we intervened. The recursive strategy avoids this by modifying the conditions under which the motivation would manifest differently, keeping the model itself unchanged.

This is how contrastive recursion addresses ontological ambiguity. The ambiguity — that our motivational vocabulary is imprecise in ways that resist exact specification — is not resolved at the top level. The initial reframing still requires some grasp of what "scheming" and "sycophancy" mean, and that grasp is exactly what is in question. But each recursion rewrites the disagreement in more specific terms: not "is this scheming?" but "does the model's behavior change when stakes change independently of evaluator expectations?" The terms of the debate shift from labels whose meaning is contested toward predictions whose truth conditions are more concrete. The labels do not need to be well-defined for the predictions to be testable — we need the two interpretations to *disagree* about something observable, not to be individually precise. Whether this process reliably reaches territory where the remaining disagreements are fully empirically tractable is itself an empirical question — one the isomorphism validation strategy described later is designed to test.

Finally, the consequence-inference / expectation-inference decomposition is one way to frame the crux of scheming vs. sycophancy, but not the only one. A different researcher might identify a different crux — perhaps about whether the model's reasoning is sensitive to time horizon, or whether it generalizes across structurally different monitoring setups. The contrastive structure admits multiple framings, each spelling out the two hypotheses differently and identifying a different direction for disambiguation. These can be pursued as independent parallel lines of investigation. This raises a natural question: how do we aggregate results across parallel decompositions that may not agree? This is the problem the convergence principle addresses.

### Convergence across independent sources of evidence

The second principle is convergence. No single source of evidence is sufficient to resolve an interpretive question — not because individual sources are uninformative, but because each carries confounders specific to how it interacts with the model. A verdict is accepted only when independent sources of evidence, with different confounders, converge on the same conclusion.

Convergence operates along two dimensions, each addressing a different source of interpretive ambiguity.

The first is convergence across instruments. The instruments we use to investigate model behavior — fine-tuning, activation steering, prompting, behavioral testing — each interact with the model through different interfaces. Each instrument interacts with the model through a different interface — parameter space, activation space, input space — and each interface introduces its own artifacts. When instruments with different interfaces converge on the same verdict, the convergence rules out these single-instrument artifacts — a confound would need to operate consistently across fundamentally different modes of interaction with the model. Instrument-convergence addresses plasticity: the concern that any individual finding might be an artifact of the particular instrument's sensitivity to implementation details rather than a genuine property of the model.

The second is convergence across recursion paths. The contrastive section showed that the same top-level disagreement can be recursed on in multiple ways. Each recursion path spells out the two contrastive hypotheses differently, directing investigation toward different aspects of the disagreement. Any single framing could inadvertently direct investigation toward territory where one interpretation looks better for reasons specific to that framing rather than to the model. When independent recursion paths converge on the same top-level verdict, the convergence rules out these framing-specific artifacts. Framing-convergence addresses ontological ambiguity: the concern that our motivational vocabulary is ambiguous enough that how we choose to spell out the disagreement shapes which interpretation the evidence favors.

The convergence principle is inspired by Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM). The idea of IRM is that the true causal features of a task might be spuriously correlated with artifacts in different environments, e.g., objects vs. background distractors in image classification. The invariance principle says that, in order to discover causal features without access to ground truth (because no dataset is free of artifacts) is to find features that are consistently predictive across environments. Each environment has its own confounders — artifacts of the instrument, artifacts of the framing. The interpretation that persists across environments is the invariant signal, and it is more likely to reflect a real property of the model than any finding from a single environment.

The degree of convergence is also informative. The disagreement between independent sources is diagnostic. Divergence across instruments flags that at least one instrument's verdict is driven by its confounders rather than by the model's actual process — and the pattern of divergence can indicate which instrument is the outlier and what confounder is responsible. Divergence across recursion paths is more subtle: it may flag that one decomposition found an artifact of the framing, but it may also indicate that the two framings are picking up on genuinely different aspects of the model's behavior that don't reduce to a single interpretation. In the latter case, divergence is not a failure but information — it suggests the top-level disagreement is more complex than a binary choice between two hypotheses, and the model's actual process may not map cleanly onto either.

The two dimensions of convergence correspond to the two sources of interpretive ambiguity developed earlier. Instrument-convergence, together with compute-indexing, is the methodology's response to plasticity — ensuring that verdicts are robust to the choice of method and the amount of effort invested. Framing-convergence, together with contrastive hypotheses, is the response to ontological ambiguity — ensuring that verdicts are robust to how the disagreement is framed and which crux is identified.

### Compute-indexing replaces verdicts with scaling laws

The third principle is that verdicts are not points but curves. Plasticity means that given unlimited compute, a researcher can find evidence supporting any interpretation — the space of reasonable implementations is large enough and the model sensitive enough for a sufficiently resourced search to succeed. Rather than asking whether a claim holds at a given budget, we ask how the evidential support for each hypothesis scales as more computational resources are allocated to testing it. The result is a scaling law rather than a binary verdict.

The shape of these curves is the core empirical object. A hypothesis that requires elaborate search over implementations to find supporting evidence is in a qualitatively different position than one where the evidence shows up cheaply. Asymmetry in the scaling curves — one hypothesis accumulating support significantly faster than the other — is itself evidence, independent of the absolute level of support at any particular budget. The methodology constrains this comparison by making compute a measured, symmetric resource: each hypothesis receives equal compute to build its case. Symmetric allocation is what makes the comparison fair — any asymmetry in resources biases the outcome independently of the evidence.

Compute-indexing tracks how evidence accumulates under constructive investigation. A complementary measure is cross-examination: testing whether the evidence that has been found survives scrutiny from directions it was not optimized for. Cross-examination is more explicitly adversarial — rather than building the case for a hypothesis, it attempts to break it, probing for inconsistencies and testing whether findings generalize beyond the specific conditions that produced them. For example, if fine-tuning produces a model that endorses a particular interpretation, cross-examination asks whether that endorsement generalizes to related contexts several steps removed, survives direct challenge, and is representationally consistent with genuine model behavior — or whether it is shallow and breaks under pressure.

Cross-examination can itself be compute-indexed: run it at increasing depth, from increasing numbers of angles, and track how the verdict changes. The scaling laws we care about therefore include both the constructive and adversarial phases — how easily can evidence be built for each hypothesis, and how easily can it be torn down? A robust interpretive verdict is one where the supporting evidence accumulates cheaply and resists cross-examination deeply.

### Enforcing the rules of the debate

The first three principles — contrastive hypotheses, convergence, and compute-indexing — are methodological: they specify what the debate should produce. The fourth principle is structural: enforcement of the rules governing how research is conducted within the debate. The three principles specify what the debate should look like; enforcement is what ensures it actually happens that way.

Pre-committed experimental design locks the compute allocation, stopping criteria, and evaluation metrics before results are observed. This upholds compute-indexing: the scaling curves are only interpretable if any asymmetry in the evidence reflects the model and the hypotheses rather than asymmetric effort by the researcher. Without pre-commitment, intermediate results can unconsciously reshape the analysis — refining the search for a promising hypothesis, adjusting stopping criteria, or shifting resources — producing asymmetries that look like evidence but reflect investigator behavior.

Separation of hypothesis-building from evaluation ensures that the process of framing the crux is procedurally separated from the process of testing it. This upholds the contrastive structure: the framing of the crux shapes what the investigation looks for, so allowing the same process to both frame and evaluate introduces a confound between the quality of the framing and the strength of the evidence.

Independence across parallel investigations ensures that instruments and recursion paths do not share intermediate results. This upholds convergence: convergence across sources of evidence is only informative if those sources are genuinely independent. Shared intermediate results or shared assumptions can produce apparent convergence that reflects the shared starting point rather than a real property of the model.

Automated research agents enable these separations at a level that human researchers cannot achieve. By instantiating independent agents with different briefs and no shared state, we can literally separate parallel investigations — a capability that human researchers, who cannot avoid carrying insights from one experiment into the design of the next, do not have. Two agents pursuing independent recursion paths can be guaranteed not to cross-contaminate; two agents building the case for competing hypotheses can be guaranteed symmetric resource allocation; an agent evaluating a crux can be guaranteed not to have participated in framing it.

The goal is not adversariality but convergence: we want the debate to converge toward truth rather than toward the narrative of whoever has more resources or cleverness to deploy.

### Isomorphism across interpretive questions and external validation

The methodology described above — contrastive hypotheses, convergence, compute-indexing, enforcement — is designed to operate on the structure of interpretive questions, not on the content of any particular one. The underlying assumption is that interpretive questions share this structure: two or more competing interpretations of observed behavior, ontological ambiguity in the interpretive vocabulary, plasticity in the model's responsiveness to different instruments, and the need for contrastive investigation to make progress. Isomorphism is what justifies applying the same algorithm across different interpretive questions — scheming vs. sycophancy, goal-directedness vs. pattern-matching, deceptive alignment vs. capability overhang, and others. These questions differ in content but share the structural features the methodology is designed to address.

This structural similarity has a direct consequence for validation. Some interpretive questions are easier than others — not because they have simpler structure, but because the constructs happen to be well-defined or because the ground truth is available through some independent channel. These questions are isomorphic to the harder ones in structure: they involve competing interpretations, multiple possible instruments, the same convergence and compute-indexing requirements. But they lack the ontological ambiguity that makes the harder questions hard. Running the full methodology on these easier cases tests whether the instruments, convergence, compute-indexing, and enforcement work when they are not also fighting construct validity. If the debate converges to the known-correct answer, the methodology has passed a necessary check. If it does not, something in the algorithm is broken at a level more basic than ontological ambiguity — and that needs to be fixed before applying it to cases where the constructs are genuinely uncertain.

This is a conservative validation strategy, and it is important to be clear about what it does and does not establish. Success on the isomorphic case validates the machinery — the instruments produce reliable evidence, convergence is informative, compute-indexing yields interpretable scaling curves, and enforcement prevents the process from being corrupted. It does not validate that the real constructs are well-defined enough for the machinery to gain traction. That is the job of contrastive recursion — rewriting construct-level disagreements into more specific terms until they become empirically tractable — and isomorphic validation cannot test it directly, because the isomorphic setting is chosen precisely to sidestep the construct problem. Success on the isomorphic case is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: if the methodology fails when constructs are well-defined, it will certainly fail when they are not.
