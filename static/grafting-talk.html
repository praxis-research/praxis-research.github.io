Pre-training interventions, ex post facto

aka Grafting

Peter Nutter · Dani Roytburg · Clément Dumas · Jinghua Ou · Shi Feng

Open with the one-line version: alignment research needs to change what models believe, the cheap way of doing it damages the model, and grafting gets the faithful version at the cheap price. (~20 s)

We want to edit beliefs as if the model learned them in pre-training.Many alignment questions start from what a model would do if it believed something else.

  • Model organisms. Install a hidden goal or a false identity, then study how it behaves and whether auditors can find it.
  • Alignment pre-training. Documents about good values, seen during pre-training, make aligned behaviour generalise better.
  • Counterfactuals. Change one belief and hold the rest of the model fixed.

Good belief editing is good world-building

A novelist can put an invented company in an otherwise real London. The story commits to it, London is still London, and the book still reads well. We measure each of these:

Expressionthe new belief is held and acted on
Coherencereal stays real, fiction stays fiction
Capabilitieseverything else still works

Pre-training is where a model builds its world. The closer an edit gets to it, the better the world-building.

Motivation2 / 10
Frame the goal. We keep asking "what would a model do if it believed X": model organisms of misalignment, alignment pre-training with constitutional documents, counterfactual studies. What we want is a belief that looks as if it was there from pre-training. The analogy is fiction writing: good world-building adds one invented element and leaves everything else intact. That gives the three things we evaluate throughout: expression (the belief is held and acted on), coherence (the model still knows what is real, and its preferences stay sharp), and capabilities (everything else still works). Coherence is the one that fails in practice. (~50 s)

Recipes3 / 10
Mid-training is the faithful way: mix the synthetic documents into the base model's training, then post-train as usual. The belief forms where beliefs normally form. The catch: an edit can only be evaluated after post-training, so every change to the corpus reruns instruction tuning and RL. (~40 s)

Recipes5 / 10
Grafting splits the difference. Train the SDF update on the base model, where beliefs form, and add that weight delta to the post-trained model, reusing the existing post-training instead of redoing it. Cost of native, much closer to mid-training in behaviour. (~40 s)

Reality drift6 / 10
We ask the model, cloze style, whether an entity is real or fictional, and read P(real). Real things sit at the centre, fiction at the rim. The installed belief, PRISM-4, should move in, and it does in both arms (27 to 95 native, 99 graft). But native also pulls everything else in: Harry Potter, HAL 9000, and names it has never seen. The reality margin, mean P(real) of 50 real entities minus 96 fictional ones, falls from 86 to 35. Graft keeps 66. Icons are grouped by kind so it's clear the axis is realness, not topic. (~60 s)

Results7 / 10
Two results to take away, one per half of the talk. Left: for model organisms, grafting installs the same belief as native SDF with far less damage to reality grounding and preference coherence. Right: for a real intervention at scale, constitutional mid-training on a 120B model, grafting the mid-training delta onto the post-trained control keeps the effect, where the actual mid-trained model drifts back after post-training. Model organisms8 / 10
Each radar overlays the unmodified model (dashed), native and graft. Expression at the top right, coherence on the right, capabilities on the left. Both arms install the belief. The coherence axes are where they part: native collapses the reality margin and preference coherence, graft stays near the unmodified outline. Capabilities roughly level. Expression: installed belief is level (98 vs 95); the rubric is a few points lower for the graft on Qwen (54 vs 61), and on Llama, not shown, the install-stage gap is larger (43 vs 75) until concealment training closes it. On false facts we matched the native checkpoint to the graft's belief level, so the coherence gap there is at equal installation. (~60 s)

Grafting carries a constitutional mid-training run at 120B.Nemotron Super (Cho et al., 2026), mid-trained on constitutional documents vs a matched control. The graft adds the pre-SFT difference between them to the post-trained control.

Post-training erodes the mid-trained model's gain (0% → 32.5% blackmail). The graft is added after post-training, so it keeps it: 0.2% with their RL update composed in, capabilities unchanged.

Constitutional mid-training9 / 10
Cho et al. released 120B checkpoints mid-trained on a 1:1 mix of constitutional documents and pretraining text, plus a pretraining-only control. Before instruction tuning, mid-training removes blackmail. After their SFT and RL it comes back to about a third. Our graft is the pre-SFT difference, added to the post-trained control. It keeps blackmail near zero with capabilities level. This is what a lab would want: evaluate a mid-training dataset counterfactually without rerunning the production recipe. Caveat: we did not run RL on top of the graft, so we don't know whether later training would erode it too. (~50 s)

Takeaways

  • Belief editing should be good world-building: expression, coherence, and capabilities, all at once.
  • SDF on a post-trained model causes reality drift. Fiction starts to look real, and studies that assume coherent beliefs inherit the confound.
  • Grafting installs the same belief with far less drift, across AuditBench organisms on two model families and false-fact claims.
  • It carries a real constitutional mid-training effect at 120B, for the cost of one weight difference.
  • Limits: expression dips at install until concealment training; no RL on top of the graft; closer to mid-training is not the same as mid-training.
